Why the numbers need care

Five tracking problems explain almost every apparent drop. Each problem comes with the evidence we found.

91% → 27%
share of engaged sessions, May 2025 against Oct 2025

The new website broke the tracking

In June 2025 the old addresses (/list/, /detail/) disappeared and new ones took their place. In the same month tracking changed completely: the share of engaged sessions dropped from around 90% to below 30%, and homepage views from over 5,000 to around 1,500 a month.

So: Google Analytics numbers from before and after June 2025 can’t be compared.

47,696
‘direct’ sessions in February 2026, normally around 3,000

Automated visitors are counted

A bot is an automated program, not a real visitor. They arrive in waves (November 2025, February–March 2026, July 2026) and come from data centres such as North Bergen, Frankfurt, Santa Clara and Ashburn, not from Belgium.

So: total visits, direct traffic and page views are not usable.

0
requests counted as a goal, January 2025 to October 2026

Requests are not measured

There is no goal set up at all and no thank-you page. Clicks on a venue’s contact button were counted between June 2025 and March 2026 (around 15 to 30 a month) and then stopped completely. The contact page fell from 436 to 11 views because the form works differently on the new website.

So: we can’t say whether you received more or fewer requests.

5,267 → 122
visits from ads, Jan–Oct 2025 against 2026

The ads stopped in September 2025

Until August 2025 Google ads brought around 450 to 690 visits a month. After that, almost nothing. Spend, clicks and cost per click are outside this report.

So: 2026 has no paid traffic to compare with.

1.34 → 1.16
organic visits in Analytics per click in Search Console, before and after July 2026

Since July 2026 Analytics misses more visitors

Search Console is the program in which Google itself counts the clicks to the website. From July 2025 Analytics logged around 1.34 visits per click (it also includes Bing and other search engines). Since July 2026 only 1.16: around 14% of visitors drop out, probably because of a change to the cookie banner.

So: Analytics numbers since July 2026 are too low.

44.7%
of Semrush’s estimated visits comes from one keyword

The Semrush estimate leans on one keyword

Semrush shows +124.6% organic visits (November 2026 against November 2025). Almost half comes from ‘kasteel ooidonk’, where you now rank first. Search Console, which counts real clicks, sees no jump. Over January to October Semrush gives −1%.

So: Semrush numbers show direction, not size.

How far you can trust each number

Reliable: Google counts it itself. Directional: an estimate, or tracking that depends on cookies. Not usable: inflated by bots, or the tracking is broken.

The evidence in four charts

Did you actually grow, but we couldn’t see it?

A common argument: ‘tracking got worse, so growth is bigger than it looks’. It only holds under strict conditions.

  1. The number can only be under-counted, never over-counted. Visits and page views drop out: bots inflate them.
  2. The drop in tracking is proven and dated. That only holds for organic visits since July 2026 (−14%).
  3. There is a source that doesn’t depend on cookies to compare with. For organic search that’s Search Console, and it says: unchanged.

Corrected for the tracking drop, organic visits in Analytics for July–October 2026 come to 7,800 to 7,900 instead of 6,761. That doesn’t change the verdict: organic search is steady, between −3% and −1%. We can say nothing about requests until your own count is in.

What search engines find on the new website

Semrush ran a crawler (a program that walks through the website) over eventonline.be. We checked its findings against Search Console, Google’s own data. The picture is more nuanced: Google does reach most individual venue pages. The real problem is the thousands of near-identical pages around them.

76%
of the roughly 1,350 individual venue pages in the sitemap got an impression in six months (approximate)

Google does reach the individual venue pages

The Semrush crawler found no venue, supplier or news page, but that doesn’t match what Google shows. The Antwerp listing page links to each venue with a normal link, and in Search Console roughly three in four individual venue pages (about 1,000 of the 1,350 we could read in the sitemap) had impressions between May and November; 18% got a click. Dutch venue pages do best (89% appeared), German ones worst (46%, 3% with a click). The bigger issue is the sitemap itself: about 16,000 of its roughly 17,600 venue and supplier addresses are type-and-town combinations such as ‘kasteel-meise’ or ‘hotels-genk’. Fewer than one in four appears in search and about 1% got a click. Google also shows about 1,300 pages under two addresses (with and without a trailing slash) and about 3,500 addresses from the old site structure, which splits their impressions. Counts are approximate: we told individual pages from combinations by their address pattern.

So: the individual pages are found. What weighs the sitemap down is thousands of near-copies that mostly go nowhere. The Search Console indexing report will show how Google treats them.

430
filter variants that soak up the crawl, each with a language-link error

Filters soak up the attention

Every category (co-working, catering, …) creates its own address, in four languages: 89 variants per language for suppliers and 17 for venues. They all point to the listing page as the main version, but their language links (hreflang) are wrong. So the crawl budget goes to copies instead of venues.

So: search engines spend their time on pages that should never appear in Google.

0
pages with structured data (schema.org)

Google gets no structured information

Structured data tells Google in a fixed format what a page is: a venue, an address, a capacity, a business. Not one page has it. For a platform with thousands of venues, that leaves rich search results and visibility in AI answers on the table.

So: competitors with structured data stand out more in search results.

19 / 26
main pages without a description for Google

Titles and descriptions are generic or missing

The homepage is called ‘Homepage | Event Online’ in all four languages. The French venue page is called ‘Lieux de evenements’ instead of ‘Lieux d’événements’. Privacy and cookies sit on technical addresses such as /node/107478 in German, English and French.

So: fewer people click through, even when you rank well.

4 in 5
searches are about one specific venue, not about ‘party venue’ or ‘event location’

The demand sits exactly on the pages that can’t be reached

In Sherlock’s keyword list, 1,007 keywords have a known search volume, about 501,550 searches a month together. At least 80% of that is venue names: Antwerp Expo (27,100), Handelsbeurs Antwerpen (18,100), Nekkerhal Mechelen (14,800), Trixxo Arena (12,100). General searches such as ‘feestzalen’ (6,600) or ‘trouwlocaties’ (1,600) make up about 18%. Someone searching for a venue name only lands on your site if that venue page ranks high, and for most it doesn’t yet.

So: findable venue pages are the biggest growth lever, bigger than new blog posts. Search volumes are estimates.

52 / 1,518
venue-page keywords in the top 3, out of all keywords venue pages rank for

Venue pages carry the site, but rarely rank at the top

Semrush’s ranking list (16 November 2026) shows that Google does know the venue pages, probably through the sitemap. They bring 3,501 of the 4,297 estimated visits (81%), almost half of that through Kasteel Ooidonk alone. But of their 1,518 keywords only 52 are in the top 3; 312 are at positions 4 to 10 and 240 at 11 to 20. Across the whole site 716 keywords sit at positions 4 to 20, worth 341,780 searches a month: Van der Valk Beveren (14,800, position 8), Handelsbeurs Antwerpen (8,100, position 9), Trixxo Arena (6,600, position 7). On 403 keywords two or more of your own pages compete with each other; for Handelsbeurs Antwerpen there are three.

So: the pages exist and Google knows them. With internal links, one page per venue and structured data they can move from the bottom of page 1 to the top 3.

Good news: pages load quickly (the HTML in 0.44 seconds), Open Graph is on every main page and there is an llms.txt for AI assistants. Each page does load 17 script and style files, 2,166 KB together. Source: Semrush Site Audit, export of 17 November 2026.

The town pages exist, but aren’t about the town

EventOnline has pages such as /nl/eventlocaties/kortemark/ and /nl/eventlocaties/gent/. But they all show the full list of about 406 venues, just sorted differently: the Ghent page ends with venues in Nice, Frankfurt and Amsterdam, and the Kortemark page isn’t even sorted. Any place name produces such a page, and the sitemap lists about 25,700 addresses, with combinations of category and place in four languages. The ‘Place’ filter only knows provinces and countries.

So: to Google, thousands of town pages are copies of each other, so it picks none of them.

How to read this

This is the core of the answer. A page for a town only works if it is about that town: the venues there and nearby, how far they are, what they cost. Today every town page is the same national list in a different order. Google recognises that and ranks a competitor’s page that really is about Kortemark.
